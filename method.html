Research practice

Method and Sources

Sattva Intelligence distinguishes textual evidence, interpretation, comparison, and contemporary application. The kind of verification required depends upon the kind of claim being made.

About the Method

Sattva Intelligence is a research and public-humanities project devoted to close reading, philosophical interpretation, and interdisciplinary inquiry involving classical Indic texts and contemporary questions.

The project begins from a basic methodological commitment: the kind of evidence required should correspond to the kind of claim being made. Establishing what a Sanskrit passage states is different from interpreting its philosophical significance. Comparing that interpretation with another intellectual tradition is different again from developing a contemporary argument from it.

These activities can inform one another, but they should not be silently collapsed.

Governing standard. The project seeks interpretations that are textually accountable, contextually informed, philosophically precise, and explicit about the boundary between textual evidence and contemporary argument.

1. Textual Method and Sources

Beginning with the Text

Work begins with an identifiable primary-text passage.

For the Bhagavata Purana series, each episode is assigned a defined textual range by canto and chapter. Chapter boundaries, speakers, narrative levels, and sequence are treated as part of the evidence. Material from a later passage should not be used to complete an argument that the assigned passage itself leaves unresolved unless that movement beyond the passage is explicitly identified.

The Sanskrit is consulted where the wording of a term, phrase, grammatical construction, or conceptual distinction materially affects the interpretation. Sanskrit is not introduced simply to give an argument greater authority.

Where an argument does not depend upon the Sanskrit wording, a reliable translation may be sufficient.

Translation Sources

The Bhagavata Purana project currently works principally with the following translations.

Edwin F. Bryant. Bhakti Yoga: Tales and Teachings from the Bhagavata Purana. North Point Press, 2017. Bryant is used where this selective anthology contains the passage under discussion.

G. V. Tagare, trans. The Bhāgavata-Purāṇa. Motilal Banarsidass. Tagare provides complete textual coverage and is used for passages outside Bryant's anthology and for independent comparison where appropriate.

Gita Press English translation. Used as an additional traditional translation source where further comparison is useful.

For Canto 10, Bryant's complete translation, Krishna: The Beautiful Legend of God, published by Penguin Classics, is also used.

This hierarchy is functional rather than doctrinal. Priority does not mean that one translation is presumed correct in every disputed passage. The appropriate source depends upon the question being investigated.

Where an episode draws upon more than one translation because its textual range extends beyond the coverage of the principal source, that mixed-source character is disclosed.

Text, Commentary, Scholarship, and Living Tradition

Different sources perform different evidentiary functions.

A primary text supplies textual evidence. A translation mediates that text into another language. A commentary supplies an interpretation situated within a particular history of reception. Modern scholarship may provide historical, literary, philosophical, philological, or comparative analysis.

Living traditions constitute another important form of reception. They may illuminate how texts and concepts continue to be understood and practiced, but living reception is not automatically evidence for what an earlier Sanskrit passage states.

Jayadayal Goyandka's Tattvachintamani, for example, is used sparingly as a tradition-voice source. It may illustrate how a living tradition teaches or receives a question. It is not used as evidence for the wording of the Bhagavata Purana and is not an authority for Sanskrit derivation.

These forms of evidence may inform one another. They should not be silently substituted for one another.

2. Sanskrit, Translation, and Terminology

Sanskrit terminology is retained when the original term contributes something that an English equivalent cannot adequately preserve.

The project does not assume, however, that a Sanskrit word possesses one fixed philosophical meaning across texts and traditions.

Terms such as dharma, buddhi, citta, ātman, yoga, bhakti, jñāna, viveka, guṇa, and mokṣa have histories of use. Their meanings may vary according to textual context, genre, philosophical school, theological commitment, and commentarial tradition.

The relevant methodological question is therefore not merely, "What does this Sanskrit word mean?" It is: What does this term mean here, in this passage, within this argument or narrative, and within this textual tradition?

A definition appropriate to the Yoga Sutras, for example, should not automatically be transferred to the Bhagavad Gita or Bhagavata Purana merely because the same Sanskrit word occurs in each.

Translation as Interpretation

Translations are treated as interpretive witnesses rather than transparent substitutes for the Sanskrit.

Translation requires choices. A Sanskrit term may possess a semantic range wider than a single English equivalent. Syntax may permit competing readings. Technical vocabulary may carry associations that ordinary English obscures.

Differences between translations are therefore examined when they materially affect an argument. This does not mean that every passage requires comparison among every available translation.

The governing principle is proportional scrutiny.

Etymology and Usage

Etymology is evidence about a word's formation, not a substitute for evidence about its use.

An attractive derivation does not by itself establish what a term means in a particular philosophical text. Interpretive claims must remain accountable to actual usage and context.

Sanskrit should therefore not function as ornament or as an automatic marker of authenticity.

Public Presentation

Scholarly pages may use standard transliteration with diacritics where precision requires it. Spoken presentations may use simplified Roman spelling for teleprompter readability.

This is a presentation distinction, not a difference in textual standard.

The future Sattva Intelligence Sanskrit and Conceptual Lexicon will follow the same principle. It will document contextual usages rather than reduce complex terms to equations such as dharma = duty or yoga = union.

3. Levels of Claim

Sattva Intelligence distinguishes four levels of claim.

The purpose is not to turn interpretation into a mechanical procedure. It is to make clear what kind of claim is being made and what kind of evidence would justify it.

Textual Claim

A textual claim concerns what a source states, describes, narrates, or explicitly attributes to a speaker.

It should remain accountable to the relevant passage.

A textual claim does not by itself establish that the position described by the text is historically factual, philosophically correct, or applicable beyond its context.

Interpretive Claim

An interpretive claim concerns what a passage may mean, what function it performs within an argument or narrative, or how a concept should be understood within its intellectual setting.

Interpretation moves beyond description, but it is not arbitrary. Its strength depends upon the textual, contextual, linguistic, philosophical, historical, or commentarial evidence supporting it.

Comparative Claim

A comparative claim places a text, concept, or argument in relation to another tradition, source, or contemporary discipline.

Resemblance can justify investigation. It does not by itself establish conceptual identity, historical influence, empirical confirmation, or anticipation.

Contemporary Application

A contemporary application asks what significance might emerge when classical material is brought into conversation with a present ethical, social, environmental, technological, political, or intellectual problem.

Such applications are contemporary arguments.

A classical concept may provide resources for thinking about artificial intelligence without the classical source itself containing a theory of artificial intelligence.

Four Questions

For significant claims, the project asks:

  1. What does the text actually say?
  2. What interpretation is being proposed?
  3. What exactly is being compared, and where are the differences?
  4. What part of the conclusion belongs to the present inquiry rather than to the classical source?

Movement between these levels is expected. The methodological requirement is that the movement remain visible.

4. Comparative Method

Sattva Intelligence places classical Indic sources in conversation with contemporary philosophy, psychology, communication studies, cognitive science, ethics, technology, environmental thought, and other fields where comparison is intellectually warranted.

The purpose of comparison is to clarify questions, identify differences in assumptions, and investigate possible convergence, tension, or conceptual contrast.

Comparison is not validation.

A resemblance between a classical Indic concept and a contemporary theory does not establish that the two are identical. It does not establish historical influence. It does not demonstrate that the classical tradition anticipated a modern scientific discovery. Nor does contemporary scientific evidence automatically verify the philosophical or theological claims of an ancient text.

The reverse problem also matters. The antiquity of an Indic concept does not confer authority upon a contemporary theory merely because the two appear similar.

Modern scholarship is therefore an interlocutor, not a tribunal before which classical texts must be scientifically validated.

The purpose of comparison is neither to modernize classical texts nor to use them to confer antiquity upon contemporary ideas. It is to permit disciplined intellectual encounter while preserving difference.

5. Difference Within Indic Traditions

"Indic thought" is used by Sattva Intelligence as a broad descriptive category, not as the name of a single philosophical system.

South Asian intellectual traditions contain substantial disagreements concerning knowledge, selfhood, consciousness, causation, language, moral action, devotion, liberation, and ultimate reality. Shared vocabulary does not erase those disagreements.

A term appearing in two texts therefore cannot automatically be assumed to perform the same philosophical work in both.

This becomes particularly important as the project expands beyond the Bhagavata Purana into the Bhagavad Gita, Yoga Sutras, Upanishads, Vedānta traditions, Sāṃkhya, Nyāya, Buddhist traditions, Kashmir Śaivism, and other bodies of thought.

Where traditions agree, the grounds of agreement should be identified. Where they disagree, the disagreement should remain visible.

The same principle applies to commentary. A later commentator may provide an influential interpretation of an earlier text without that interpretation becoming identical with the wording of the earlier source.

The aim is not to manufacture an underlying unity. It is to make intellectual comparison possible without erasing difference.

6. Verification, Citation, and Editorial Transparency

Scholarly reliability depends not merely upon having citations, but upon whether the evidence cited actually supports the claim being made.

The central question is therefore not simply, "Is there a citation?" It is: Does this source support this claim at the level at which the claim is being made?

A translation can establish how a translator renders a passage. It cannot by itself establish the historical development of a doctrine. A commentary can document an interpretive tradition without proving that its reading was the only possible meaning of the earlier text. A scientific study can establish an empirical finding without demonstrating that an apparently similar classical concept means the same thing.

The level of verification should match the level of the claim.

Citation and Traceability

References should allow readers, where practicable, to identify and examine the evidentiary basis of significant claims.

For classical texts, stable textual references such as canto, chapter, verse, or sutra are preferred where appropriate because pagination varies between editions.

Episode companion pages identify the textual range, principal sources, significant passages, interpretive questions, corrections, and relevant comparative material so that the evidentiary path remains visible.

Narrative Evidence

Narrative reconstruction requires evidentiary discipline.

Evidence that two events occurred does not necessarily establish their exact temporal relationship, causal connection, or the motivation of the participants. Plausible narrative bridges should not be converted into textual facts simply because they produce smoother storytelling.

Where the source leaves something unstated, the project should resist filling the gap unless the inference is identified as such.

Uncertainty

Uncertainty is a legitimate scholarly result.

Where evidence supports several reasonable readings, those alternatives may be retained. Where evidence is insufficient, confidence should not be manufactured through prose.

At the same time, qualification should not become automatic vagueness. Clear evidence permits clear conclusions.

The goal is calibrated confidence.

Corrections and Revision

Errors are possible. Scholarly accountability includes the capacity to correct them.

Substantive errors involving textual attribution, Sanskrit terminology, translation, narrative sequence, bibliography, factual information, or interpretation should be corrected transparently when they materially affect the record.

The project already follows this practice. The Episode 3 editorial record, for example, documents that an earlier draft had moved later material concerning Brahma's vision and resolution into Chapter 5. The episode was corrected before recording so that the unresolved endpoint of Chapter 5 remained intact.

The Corrections Log records substantive corrections that affect the public scholarly record.

A distinction is maintained between correction and revision.

Correction addresses inaccuracy.

Revision records intellectual development, such as a changed interpretation resulting from further reading or new evidence.

A long-term research project should be capable of doing both without treating every development of thought as an earlier factual error.

7. Scope, Standards, and Limitations

Sattva Intelligence combines textual interpretation, philosophical analysis, interdisciplinary comparison, and public scholarship.

It is not a critical edition of the Sanskrit texts. It does not undertake systematic manuscript collation, establish a new Sanskrit recension, or replace specialist philological, historical, or manuscript scholarship.

Nor does the project claim to provide a single authoritative interpretation of the traditions it studies. Classical texts have histories of commentary, institutional transmission, philosophical disagreement, translation, and reception. Interpretations advanced here should therefore be assessed by their evidence and reasoning.

The project also distinguishes a text's own account from modern historical and empirical claims.

Cosmological, chronological, supernatural, and historically contested material is attributed appropriately through formulations such as "the text describes," "according to the Bhagavata Purana," or "within the text's account."

This allows a classical text's conceptual world to be examined seriously without silently converting its claims into propositions of modern historiography or natural science.

Public accessibility does not remove the obligation of scholarly accountability. Videos may simplify exposition for oral comprehension, while companion pages provide fuller citations, textual notes, interpretive qualifications, methodological explanation, and corrections.

Sattva Intelligence nevertheless advances interpretations of its own. Methodological transparency does not require pretending that the project has no intellectual position.

Transparency does not require interpretive neutrality. It requires distinguishing the evidence from the argument made from that evidence.

The governing standard is therefore not a claim to finality. It is accountability: to the text, to relevant scholarship, to differences among traditions, to the distinction between evidence and interpretation, and to the reader.
