Bhagavata Purana · Episode 8 · Textual Commentary

You Won the War. Why Isn't It Over?

Bhagavata Purana, Canto 1, Chapters 7–8

Watch Episode 8 on YouTube

About the title. The title is this project's own formulation, not a translation or quotation from the Sanskrit. It names the episode's central question rather than a specific verse: across these two chapters, military victory is achieved, but the text does not treat that victory as resolving the moral situation it produced.

Abstract

This episode examines Bhagavata Purana 1.7–1.8, moving from Vyasa's composition of the Bhagavata itself into the immediate aftermath of the Mahabharata war. Aswatthama kills Draupadi's sleeping sons and is captured by Arjuna; Draupadi, Bhima, and Krishna bring different obligations into the judgment that follows, and Arjuna acts within that tension rather than resolving it in favor of one claim alone. The threat then extends forward to Uttara's unborn child, Parikshit, whose survival the text presents as dependent on Krishna's direct protection. Kunti's prayer interprets a lifetime of repeated danger through dependence on Krishna, while Yudhishthira, victorious but unconsoled, cannot accept the conventional justification for the war he has won. The episode reads these two chapters as staging two different directions of judgment: outward, toward a person who caused harm, and inward, toward the person who caused harm to be necessary at all.

Central Question

What happens when winning the war does not settle the moral problem? The episode follows how the Bhagavata raises this question through two distinct movements, Arjuna's judgment of Ashvatthama and Yudhishthira's judgment of himself, without treating either as fully resolved by the chapter's end.

Textual Scope

Bhagavata Purana 1.7.1-12 — The book enters its own story

Following Narada's diagnosis in the previous episode, Vyasa retreats to the bank of the Sarasvati and enters meditation. The text describes him perceiving the Supreme Being and maya, and understanding both the condition of suffering produced by that association and its resolution through devotion. Vyasa composes the Bhagavata Purana and teaches it to his son, Shuka. The sages then ask why Shuka, already established beyond ordinary attachment, would study the text at all; Suta's answer, that even self-realized sages are drawn to Hari's qualities without external motive, is among the most widely discussed verses in the entire text. The chapter then turns toward Parikshit, reversing the narrative movement that has carried the series backward since Episode 1.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7.13-33 — The war ended, the killing did not

The narrative returns to the aftermath of the main battle. Ashvatthama kills Draupadi's sleeping sons; the Bhagavata's own account of Duryodhana's reaction to this act differs from the more familiar Mahabharata version, a divergence Tagare notes directly. Arjuna pursues Ashvatthama, who releases an uncontrollable weapon he cannot withdraw. Arjuna releases a counter-weapon, and verse 32 states that he withdraws both once the wider danger becomes clear, before capturing Ashvatthama. The weapon in this chapter is not redirected toward Uttara; that is a separate, later event in Chapter 8.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7.34-52 — Draupadi does not give one reason

Krishna's initial instruction invokes limits on whom a person who understands dharma may kill, then reminds Arjuna of his promise to Draupadi. Verse 40 states that Krishna was testing Arjuna's sense of dharma. Draupadi, presented with the captive, offers a multi-part argument for restraint: Ashvatthama's relationship to his father, Arjuna's teacher; the grief of Ashvatthama's mother; and the wider social consequences of violence against a Brahmana. Yudhishthira and others approve her reasoning; Bhima objects, arguing that the killing of sleeping, defenseless children warrants death.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7.53-58 — A judgment under competing obligations

Verse 53 gives Krishna's resolution: a fallen Brahmana should not be killed, yet the offense still requires punishment. Verse 55 states that Arjuna understands this intention; he removes Ashvatthama's crest-jewel and hair and expels him from the camp. Verse 57 frames this, in Tagare's translation, as a form of capital punishment appropriate to an unworthy Brahmana, in place of physical execution. The chapter closes with funeral rites for the dead.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.1-16 — The future is still under attack

As Krishna prepares to leave for Dvaraka, Uttara appeals to him for protection, fearing for her unborn child, Parikshit, the same figure introduced at the start of Chapter 7 and first encountered in Episode 1. This is a second, distinct threat from Ashvatthama, separate from the confrontation already resolved in Chapter 7. Verse 14 states that Hari covers the embryo through his maya; verse 15 names the weapon again as Brahma-shiras and describes it becoming quiescent before Vishnu's power. The child survives.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.17-44 — Kunti after catastrophe

Kunti addresses Krishna in theological terms, then turns to memory, comparing her situation to Devaki's and cataloguing a series of past dangers in verse 24. Verse 25, one of the most quoted verses in the entire Bhagavata Purana, asks that calamity continue if it brings the sight of Krishna, a request the episode reads within the surrounding verses' logic of dependence and remembrance, not as a general instruction to seek suffering. Kunti reflects on pride, identifies Krishna with Time, and offers several accounts (1.8.30-35) of why Krishna acts in the world. Her prayer closes by asking that her attachment to family be replaced by attachment to Krishna himself, using the image of the Ganges flowing to the sea.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.44-52 — The victor who cannot be consoled

Yudhishthira, already advised by Vyasa and Krishna, remains overcome by grief. Verse 50 states the conventional precept that killing in a righteous war is not sinful for a protecting king; Yudhishthira knows this argument and is not consoled by it. Tagare's translation adds a parenthetical explanation for this, noted here as the translator's own gloss rather than the verse's bare content. Verse 51 turns to the suffering of war widows. The chapter closes on an analogy: killing cannot be expiated by further killing, as muddy water cannot be purified by adding more mud. Chapter 8 ends without resolving Yudhishthira's crisis; Chapter 9 opens with him seeking Bhishma's instruction on dharma.

Argument and Interpretation

The episode reads these two chapters as staging two different directions of moral judgment. In Chapter 7, judgment is directed outward: Arjuna must decide what is owed to a person who has caused terrible harm, with Draupadi, Bhima, and Krishna each bringing a distinct, serious obligation into that decision. Arjuna's resolution, punishment without execution, answers several of these claims at once rather than privileging one.

In Chapter 8, judgment turns inward. Yudhishthira has won the war and the kingdom, yet he holds himself responsible for the human cost that victory required. The conventional dharmic justification for killing in a righteous war is available to him and does not resolve his conscience. This is this project's own interpretive framing of the movement across the two chapters; the Bhagavata itself does not state the contrast in these terms.

Two moments in the text resist being flattened into simpler modern equivalents. Draupadi's response to Ashvatthama is not adequately described as forgiveness alone; it is a constructed argument from several distinct relationships and obligations at once. And verse 25 of Kunti's prayer is not a general endorsement of suffering; it is conditioned entirely on suffering's capacity to bring the sight of Krishna, within a larger prayer that ends by asking for release from attachment, not for further calamity as such.

Key Terms

Brahma-shiras. The weapon released by Ashvatthama, named specifically at 1.7.14 and again at 1.8.15 for the second, distinct incident in Chapter 8. The text is explicit that Ashvatthama can release the weapon but does not know how to withdraw it, a capacity Arjuna possesses in the Chapter 7 confrontation.

Maya. Introduced in this episode both in Vyasa's own vision (1.7) and in Krishna's protection of the unborn Parikshit (1.8.14). The term carries different functional roles in each context and should not be treated as a single flattened concept across both uses without attention to what each passage is actually claiming.

Verification and Editorial Record

  • Textual boundary verified as Bhagavata Purana 1.7–1.8.
  • The Chapter 7 and Chapter 8 weapon incidents are confirmed as two distinct events (verse 32 for the first, resolved without redirection toward Uttara; verses 14–15 for the second, separate threat). This distinction was specifically checked before scripting given its divergence from more familiar retellings of this episode.
  • Duryodhana's reaction to Ashvatthama's act is presented per the Bhagavata's own account, which Tagare notes differs from the Mahabharata's version. The two are not reconciled; the Bhagavata's version is reported as its own account.
  • Tagare's parenthetical explanation for Yudhishthira's rejection of the righteous-war precept (1.8.50) is attributed to Tagare specifically, not presented as the verse's own explicit content.
  • The episode does not narrate Chapter 9. Bhishma's instruction to Yudhishthira is reserved for Episode 9.
